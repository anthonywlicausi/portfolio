The New Alchemy: Turning Mercury Into Gold Is Real. And Gold May Be the Least Interesting Part.

How nuclear transmutation, fusion energy, and the emerging concept of femtotechnology could redefine the economics of scarcity.

Anthony W. Licausi October 29, 2026 7 min read

For thousands of years, alchemists pursued an impossible dream: turning ordinary metals into gold.

They failed because they were working with chemistry.

Today, physicists are approaching the same problem with nuclear science. And this time, the underlying physics actually works.

A company called Marathon Fusion is exploring a process that could convert mercury into gold using neutrons generated by nuclear fusion. Its researchers have modeled a system capable of producing significant quantities of gold as a byproduct of a functioning fusion reactor.

Yes, actual gold. The same element found in jewelry, central bank reserves, and investment portfolios.

But the gold isn’t what makes this story extraordinary.

It’s what happens when humanity begins treating the atomic nucleus itself as something we can engineer.

The Difference Between Mercury and Gold Is One Proton

Every element in the periodic table is defined by the number of protons in its nucleus.

Gold has 79. Mercury has 80.

That’s it.

The distinction between two entirely different elements, one prized for its scarcity and the other historically used in thermometers and industrial processes, comes down to a single proton.

Ordinary chemistry cannot change that number. Chemical reactions rearrange electrons and create or break molecular bonds. They do not change one element into another.

Nuclear reactions can.

This process is called nuclear transmutation, and scientists have understood its basic principles for decades.

The challenge has never been whether transmutation is physically possible. It has been whether we can accomplish it efficiently, safely, and economically enough to matter.

That’s where fusion enters the picture.

How a Fusion Reactor Could Manufacture Gold

Marathon Fusion’s proposed approach begins with mercury-198, a naturally occurring isotope of mercury.

Inside a fusion reactor, energetic neutrons interact with mercury-198 nuclei. One possible reaction ejects two neutrons and produces mercury-197, a radioactive isotope.

Mercury-197 subsequently undergoes electron capture, converting one of its protons into a neutron.

The result is gold-197.

Not synthetic gold in the sense of a laboratory imitation. Not gold plating or a chemically similar substitute.

Real, stable, naturally identical gold.

The nuclear pathway can be summarized as:

Mercury-198 → Mercury-197 → Gold-197

The important distinction is that the proposed commercial process is not yet an established industrial operation.

Producing gold through nuclear reactions is scientifically possible. Producing it profitably, in substantial quantities, as part of a commercial fusion power system remains an engineering and economic challenge.

Marathon Fusion’s research suggests that a sufficiently powerful fusion facility could potentially produce gold at a scale that makes the idea worth investigating.

That possibility alone deserves attention.

Introducing Femtotechnology: Engineering Below the Atomic Scale

Most people are familiar with nanotechnology, which involves manipulating matter at scales measured in billionths of a meter.

But there is another frontier much deeper inside matter.

A femtometer is one quadrillionth of a meter, approximately the scale of atomic nuclei.

The term femtotechnology describes the speculative ambition of engineering matter at this nuclear scale.

To understand the conceptual progression:

  • Nanotechnology works with structures at approximately 10−9 meters.
  • Picotechnology refers to scales around 10−12 meters.
  • Femtotechnology reaches approximately 10−15 meters.

These are scales, not three equally mature engineering industries. Nanotechnology is well established; femtotechnology remains a loosely defined, emerging concept.

Yet the distinction is profound.

At the nanoscale, we manipulate the arrangement of atoms.

At the nuclear scale, we can potentially alter the identity and isotopic composition of the atoms themselves.

That opens an entirely different set of possibilities.

What Happens When Scarcity Becomes an Engineering Problem?

As someone who has spent much of my career thinking about markets, technology, and the allocation of capital, this is the part I find most fascinating.

Much of our economic system is built around scarcity.

Gold has commanded value for thousands of years partly because it is difficult to obtain, costly to extract, durable, and widely trusted.

Rare isotopes are expensive because nature produces them in limited quantities or because manufacturing them requires specialized nuclear infrastructure.

Entire supply chains depend on where particular elements are found, how they are extracted, and which countries control their production.

Now consider a future in which certain scarce isotopes can be manufactured on demand.

The economics change.

Not necessarily overnight. And certainly not without enormous capital expenditure, energy requirements, regulatory oversight, and technical complexity.

But the underlying assumption shifts.

Instead of asking where a material can be found, we begin asking whether it can be manufactured.

That’s a fundamentally different economic question.

Of course, manufacturing an element does not automatically make it cheap. Energy costs, reaction efficiency, isotope separation, processing, safety, and capital intensity all matter.

And not every element or isotope will be equally practical to produce.

Nevertheless, the potential implications extend far beyond precious metals.

Gold Is the Demonstration. The Applications Could Be Much Bigger.

Consider nuclear medicine.

Certain isotopes are essential for diagnosing disease and treating cancer. Their production can depend on aging reactors, specialized accelerators, and geographically concentrated supply chains.

More flexible isotope manufacturing could improve availability and resilience.

Consider advanced energy.

Nuclear technologies depend on particular isotopes with highly specific physical properties. Better control over isotope production could create new opportunities in reactor design, fuel cycles, and supporting infrastructure.

Consider scientific instrumentation and advanced materials.

Some applications depend not merely on the chemical element involved, but on the exact isotope used.

The ability to produce those isotopes more efficiently could expand the range of commercially viable technologies.

And then there is the broader possibility of nuclear-scale engineering itself.

We should be careful not to confuse demonstrated transmutation with the ability to freely design any material from the nucleus upward. We are nowhere near that level of control.

But it is a direction worth watching.

The Investment Implications: A New Layer of Deep Technology

The most obvious investment narrative is gold production.

I suspect that may be the least interesting one.

If nuclear transmutation becomes commercially scalable, the more significant opportunities may emerge in the enabling infrastructure.

High-energy neutron sources. Advanced reactor materials. Isotope separation. Radiation-resistant components. Nuclear simulation software. Specialized manufacturing. Regulatory and safety systems.

These are not necessarily glamorous businesses.

But historically, some of the most durable opportunities in technological revolutions have emerged from the infrastructure required to make the breakthrough useful.

The semiconductor revolution created far more than a market for individual chips.

The internet created far more than websites.

Artificial intelligence is creating entire layers of infrastructure, orchestration, computing, and automation.

Commercial nuclear-scale manufacturing could follow a similar pattern, although its technical and regulatory barriers are substantially different.

For investors, the important questions are not simply whether an experiment works.

They are whether the process can scale, whether its unit economics make sense, whether customers exist, and whether the resulting business can defend its position.

A remarkable scientific demonstration is not automatically a remarkable investment.

But it can reveal where the next category of companies may emerge.

The Real Breakthrough Is a Change in How We Think About Matter

There is something extraordinary about the fact that medieval alchemists were pursuing an outcome that modern nuclear physics can actually produce.

Their methods were wrong. Their understanding of matter was incomplete.

But the ambition—to transform one element into another—was not inherently impossible.

We now know the rules governing that transformation.

The challenge is learning how to exploit them economically.

And that brings us to a much larger idea.

For most of human history, technological progress has involved discovering materials, extracting them, refining them, and rearranging them into useful forms.

Nuclear transmutation introduces another possibility: changing the elemental identity of the material itself.

We are still at an early stage of understanding whether this can become a broadly useful manufacturing capability.

But the direction is unmistakably interesting.

The future may not simply be about finding more of what nature has given us. It may increasingly be about learning to manufacture what nature has made scarce.

And if that future arrives, turning mercury into gold may ultimately be remembered as one of its more modest achievements.
